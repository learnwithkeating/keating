The Forgetting Curve and What It Predicts

Most of what you study leaks away. The useful question is not how fast, but what sets the rate.

Before you read

A committed guess before reading measurably improves what sticks, even when the guess is wrong, so answer both of these before going on; errors here are productive.

Two groups study the same passage for the same total time. One group rereads it. The other puts it away and writes down everything it can recall. Both are tested five minutes later, and again a week later. Commit to a guess: who is ahead at each of those two points?

You finish a long cram session and can recall roughly 95% of the material right then. Commit to an answer: what does that number tell you about what you will know in a month?

Why this lesson

Everyone knows that studied material fades. That knowledge changes nothing on its own, because it treats forgetting as weather. The experimental literature says something more useful and less comfortable: the rate at which material fades is largely set by what you did while studying it, and the differences between methods are large enough to be worth reorganising your habits around. This lesson establishes that claim and the measurement problem that hides it. The rest of the course is about the methods.

There is no single forgetting curve

The familiar picture is a line that starts high, drops steeply, then flattens: retention plotted against time since study. As a shape it is real. As a prediction it is incomplete, because any such line is drawn from one experiment under one set of study conditions, and the conditions are the variable that matters.

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) measured two curves in a single experiment. Students who reread the material scored 83% when tested five minutes later, and 40% when tested a week later. Students who spent the same total time recalling the material from memory scored 71% at five minutes, and 61% at a week. Two curves, from one experiment, on the same material, crossing somewhere between those two measurements.

Read that pair of numbers carefully, because it contains the whole design problem. If you had stopped the experiment at five minutes you would have concluded that rereading is the better method by twelve points. If you had run it only to one week you would have concluded that retrieval is better by twenty-one points. The ranking of the two techniques literally flips between the end of the session and one week out.

What immediate accuracy cannot tell you

The general form of this problem is the central dissociation of Bjork's new theory of disuse (Bjork & Bjork 1992; Soderstrom & Bjork 2015): conditions that raise how accessible material is right now often build durable memory slowest, and the reverse. Current performance is, in their phrase, an unreliable index of the changes that support retention and transfer.

Be precise about the status of that claim. It is a theoretical framework rather than a single measured effect, so no one effect size stands behind it. What carries the weight is that the pattern it names keeps reappearing independently: massed and blocked practice inflate immediate performance while spaced, interleaved and tested practice win at delay, and that shows up separately in the spacing literature, the interleaving literature and the testing literature. You will meet all three in this course, and you can check the pattern yourself as you go.

The practical consequence is a rule about measurement rather than about studying. Any check you run at the end of a study session is measuring accessibility, which is the thing that was just inflated by the studying. To find out whether you have learned something, the check has to happen after a delay, and without the material in front of you. That is a slower and less flattering way to run a self-assessment, and it is the only one that answers the question you actually asked.

Check your recall

Answers stay hidden until you commit an attempt. Four items, all from this lesson.

Reproduce the Roediger and Karpicke (2006) result: the two conditions, the four numbers, and what the crossover between them shows.

What does Cepeda and colleagues' (2008) finding say about the mastery you feel at the end of a massed study session, and what does that imply about when to run a self-check?

Someone summarises Bjork's position as "how well you do during practice is meaningless". Say precisely what the position does claim, and state the epistemic status the claim carries.

You have a spot quiz in ten minutes on material you just read, and a cumulative exam on the same material in six weeks. Using the Roediger and Karpicke result, say what it recommends for each case, and explain why recommending different things is not a contradiction.

Write before we talk

Closed book, three sentences at most. Name one thing you studied in the last year that you can no longer recall. Then write what you actually did while studying it, in verbs. Then predict which of this lesson's two curves your method put you on, and say what evidence you have for that prediction other than the fact that you forgot. Bring all three to the chat.
