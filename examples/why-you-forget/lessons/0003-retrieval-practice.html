Retrieval Practice and the Testing Effect

Pulling something out of memory changes the memory. Putting it back in front of your eyes mostly does not.

Before you read

A committed guess before reading measurably improves what sticks, even when the guess is wrong, so answer both of these before going on; errors here are productive.

Four independent meta-analyses have compared practising retrieval against restudying the same material for the same time. Commit to a guess at the pooled effect size, in standard deviations, where 0.2 counts as small, 0.5 as medium and 0.8 as large.

You test yourself on material, but you never check whether you were right. Commit to yes or no: does the retrieval attempt still help? Add one sentence on why.

Why this lesson

Unit 1 established the problem: forgetting rates differ by method, and the signal you feel cannot tell the methods apart. This unit is the three things that reliably work. Retrieval practice comes first because it is the best-supported intervention in the entire learning literature, and because the other two are, in part, ways of arranging it.

How large the effect is, and where it holds

Practising retrieval of studied material produces better long-term retention than restudying it for the same total time. This is the testing effect, and four independent meta-analyses converge on roughly half a standard deviation: g = 0.50 with a confidence interval of 0.42 to 0.58 across 61 studies against restudy (Rowland 2014); g = 0.61 against all controls (Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017); g = 0.499 across 222 studies and 48,478 students in real classrooms (Yang et al. 2021); and 57% of 49 classroom effect sizes medium or large (Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021). Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rate practice testing one of only two high-utility techniques out of ten, alongside distributed practice, which is Lesson 4.

The comparison is not only against passive rereading. Closed-book retrieval outperformed elaborative concept mapping on a one-week test, including on inference questions, and it held even when the final test was itself concept-map creation (Karpicke & Blunt 2011, independently replicated by Lechuga, Ortega-Tudela & Gómez-Ariza 2015). The published methodological debate about that study turns largely on whether the elaboration was done open book or closed book, which is itself the design lesson worth taking: elaboration performed from memory is already a retrieval event.

Where the effect reverses and what it requires

Four conditions and boundaries do most of the practical work.

It reverses at very short delays. This is Lesson 1's crossover, and it is the boundary most often dropped in summaries. In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), restudy beat testing at five minutes, 83% against 71%, and testing won decisively at one week, 61% against 40%. The effect grows with the retention interval. Anything you evaluate on end-of-session performance, you evaluate wrong.

It requires feedback. Without feedback, low-success recall practice loses its advantage (Kang et al. 2007); failed retrieval helps only when the answer is presented afterwards (Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009); and feedback benefits correct responses as well as incorrect ones (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2007). There is a moderator analysis suggesting that feedback combined with low success yields the largest benefits of all, but its values are correlational and could not be verified against the source, so the rule to adopt is the experimentally supported one rather than any attempt to tune the difficulty: always show the correct answer, after every attempt.

Format matters, and interacts with feedback. Recall-format initial tests yield larger testing effects than recognition tests (Rowland 2014). But without feedback, multiple choice can beat short answer simply because unaided short-answer success is low; with feedback the ranking reverses and short answer wins (Kang et al. 2007). In real classrooms both formats work (McDermott et al. 2014). Recognising a correct answer and producing it are different acts, and only one of them is what the effect is named after.

One correct recall is not learning. Once an item has been recalled successfully, further restudy adds almost nothing, while continued retrieval roughly doubles delayed recall. Dropping items from further testing after a single correct answer was devastating: about 80% retention at one week when items stayed in the testing set, against 36% when they were dropped (Karpicke & Roediger 2008). The efficiency point is around three correct recalls during initial learning, then relearning to criterion about three more times at widely spaced intervals (Rawson & Dunlosky 2011).

One boundary is genuinely unsettled, and should be held that way. Whether the testing effect shrinks or disappears for highly complex, multi-step problem solving learned from worked examples is a live dispute: van Gog and Sweller (2015) argue it weakens with element interactivity; Karpicke and Aue (2015) reply that element interactivity was never operationalised or manipulated in the studies cited. The effect is not in question for facts, vocabulary and educationally realistic prose. If what you are studying is a novel multi-step procedure, treat the transfer of this lesson to it as an open question rather than a settled one.

A last property, which Lesson 5 will call back to. Retrieval also potentiates what comes after it: testing interpolated between blocks of content improves the encoding of subsequent new material, an effect named the forward testing effect (Chan, Meissner & Davis 2018), with the caveat that interference-prone material can reverse it. The ordinary testing effect looks backward at the material tested; the forward testing effect looks ahead at material not yet studied.

Check your recall

Answers stay hidden until you commit an attempt. Three items from this lesson, one reaching back.

State the size of the testing effect and the evidence base behind that number, then say what the comparison condition is and why that matters.

Under what condition does the testing effect fail to appear, or reverse? Give the numbers, and state the methodological rule that follows for anyone evaluating a study technique.

You are working through a stack of cards and you drop each card as soon as you get it right once, on the reasoning that you already know it. Say what the evidence predicts about your retention a week later, with numbers, and describe what the schedule should be instead.

Reaching back to Lesson 2. A learner switches from rereading to self-testing and reports: "this feels much worse, and I am clearly retaining less." Separate the two claims in that sentence, say which one the evidence predicts and which one it contradicts, and name the measurement that settles the disagreement.
