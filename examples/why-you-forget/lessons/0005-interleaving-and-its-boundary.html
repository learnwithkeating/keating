Interleaving, and the Boundary Where It Reverses

Mixing problem types makes practice feel worse and go better, up to the point where the material stops being confusable. Then it goes the other way.

Before you read

A committed guess before reading measurably improves what sticks, even when the guess is wrong, so answer both of these before going on; errors here are productive.

Students practise several confusable problem types either mixed together or one type at a time. Commit to a guess: who looks better during the practice session itself, who looks better on a delayed test, and roughly how large is each gap?

Interleaving does not help everything, and for one class of material the meta-analytic effect actually favours blocking. Commit to a guess at what kind of material that is, and why it would go the other way.

Why this lesson

Interleaving is the third of the three techniques, and the one with the sharpest published boundary. That makes it the best test of whether the first four lessons took: an effect with a strong average, a strong classroom replication, and a documented condition under which the opposite treatment wins is exactly the shape of finding that summaries flatten and practitioners then misapply.

What interleaving costs and what it buys

Interleaving means practising several related kinds of problem mixed together rather than one kind at a time. Blocking is the alternative, and it is what most textbooks and most self-made study plans do by default: all the problems of one type, then all of the next.

The trade is unusually clean. In a small lab study, blocked practicers led during the practice session 89% against 60%, then lost a week later, 63% against 20% (Rohrer & Taylor 2007). Because that study was small, the stronger evidence is the preregistered classroom randomised trial with 787 students, which found 61% against 38%, d = 0.83, on a surprise test a month later (Rohrer et al. 2020).

The mechanism credited for this is discriminative contrast: learning which strategy or concept goes with which case, which you can only practise when consecutive items differ. Blocked practice tells you the type in advance, so the hardest part of the real task, deciding what kind of problem you are looking at, never gets practised. Interleaving also embeds spacing, since returning to a type later necessarily puts a gap before it, so part of Lesson 4's effect is riding along.

Note what the practice-phase numbers mean. The 89% against 60% is not noise, and it is not a small inconvenience. Someone practising this way will feel, correctly, that they are performing worse. That is the same fluency signal from Lesson 2, now appearing during practice rather than after it, and it is why this technique is abandoned more often than it is refuted.

Where interleaving stops helping and starts hurting

The meta-analysis to hold in mind is Brunmair and Richter (2019), because it reports the moderators rather than only the average. The overall effect is g = 0.42. Underneath that average:

  • g = 0.67 for visually confusable categories, the best case;
  • g = 0.34 for mathematics;
  • null for expository texts;
  • g = −0.39 for unrelated word-learning materials, which is a reversal, not an absence.

Read the last line carefully, because the sign is the whole point. A negative effect of that size is not "interleaving did not help here". It is a benefit of comparable size accruing to the other condition: for that material, blocking is the better technique by about as much as interleaving is better for mathematics. An honest reading of this meta-analysis does not produce a rule that says interleave; it produces a rule that says interleave within families of confusable concepts, and block material whose items have no confusable neighbours.

That gives you a test you can actually apply. Before mixing two things in a practice session, ask whether you have ever mistaken one for the other. If yes, they belong in the same set. If they have nothing to do with each other, mixing them is not rigour, it is just a harder-feeling session with a meta-analytic effect pointing the wrong way.

Check your recall

Answers stay hidden until you commit an attempt. Two items from this lesson, two reaching back.

Give the design and the result of the classroom trial of interleaving, and say why it is the stronger of the two studies in this lesson.

Three study plans. (a) Alternating between two statistical tests that are easy to mix up. (b) Alternating between vocabulary in an unrelated foreign language and organic chemistry mechanisms. (c) Alternating between chapters of an expository history text. For each, say whether to interleave or block, and cite the moderator that decides it.

Reaching back to Lesson 4. Three terms get used loosely: massed practice, spaced practice, distributed practice. Say which of the three name the same thing in this literature and which is the contrast condition, give the one figure that makes the contrast concrete, and then say how the manipulation in this lesson differs from all three.

Reaching back to Lesson 3. Two effects share the word testing. One says that testing material improves later memory for that same material; the other says that testing improves the learning of material studied afterwards. Name both, say which direction in time each one points, and give the caveat attached to the second.

Write before we talk

Closed book. List the concepts in whatever you are currently studying that you most often mix up with each other, in pairs or small groups. That list is your interleaving set, and it is the only part of your material this lesson recommends mixing; everything not on it should be practised blocked. Bring the list, and bring one pair you were surprised to find on it.
