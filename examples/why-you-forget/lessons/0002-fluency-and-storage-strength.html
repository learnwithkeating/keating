Fluency and Storage Strength

Rereading feels productive. The feeling is a reading on how easily the words are going down, and ease is not retention.

A systematic review rated ten common study techniques for utility. Guess where rereading, highlighting and summarization landed, and say what you think a low rating would actually mean.

Why this lesson

Lesson 1 left an open question. If the differences between study methods are that large, and they are visible in ordinary experiments, why do people keep choosing the weaker ones? The answer is not laziness. It is that the signal learners steer by, the sense that the material is going in, is generated by something other than durable memory, and it points the wrong way at exactly the moments that matter.

Two strengths, and only one of them is felt

Bjork's framework separates two things that everyday language calls knowing something. Storage strength is how well established the material is in memory over the long run. Retrieval strength, which the teaching literature also calls fluency, is how accessible it is right now. The two come apart, and the ways they come apart are systematic: conditions that raise accessibility fastest, massing, blocking, restudying, are often the ones that build storage slowest.

Carry the same hedge as last lesson. The framework is a theory rather than a single measured effect. What makes it usable is that the dissociation it predicts shows up independently in three separate literatures, which you will meet in Unit 2.

The important asymmetry is that only one of the two strengths is available to introspection. You can feel fluency directly; it is the sensation of the sentence making sense as you read it, of the answer being right there. You cannot feel storage strength at all. So when a learner asks herself whether she has learned something, the only instrument in the room is measuring the wrong quantity.

Why the illusion drives real decisions

The evidence that fluency inflates self-assessment is direct. Judgments of learning made with the answer in view are inflated (Koriat & Bjork 2005). Perceptual ease inflates them too, with little to no difference in actual recall (Rhodes & Castel 2008). And the calibration failures above are not small: uncorrelated predictions across conditions ranging from 33% to 80% recall, and a repeated-study group that predicted the best retention while performing the worst.

It would be tolerable if these judgments were merely wrong. They are not merely wrong, they are load-bearing. When judgments of learning were experimentally dissociated from actual recall, learners' restudy choices followed the illusion rather than the recall (Metcalfe & Finn 2008). Overconfident learners stop studying early and retain less (Dunlosky & Rawson 2012). The bad measurement propagates into how long you study, what you restudy, and when you decide you are done.

Against that background, the utility ratings make sense. Rereading, highlighting and summarization are rated low utility by Dunlosky and colleagues (2013), with the review's own qualification that low means low generality of benefit rather than proven harm. What these three have in common is that they raise fluency reliably and cheaply. They are not popular in spite of feeling good; they are popular because feeling good is the only feedback the learner receives.

There is a well-established fix, and it is a change of timing rather than of effort. Delay the judgment until after a retrieval attempt. Delayed judgments of learning are far more accurate than immediate ones: an accuracy advantage of g = 0.93 across 112 effect sizes (Rhodes & Tauber 2011). The mechanism is the obvious one. A judgment made with the answer visible is a report on how the answer looks. A judgment made after trying to produce the answer from memory is a report on whether you could.

Write before we talk
