Spacing, and Why the Gap Beats the Cram

The same total study time, cut into pieces with time in between, is worth substantially more. The size of the gap is the design decision.

Before you read

A committed guess before reading measurably improves what sticks, even when the guess is wrong, so answer both of these before going on; errors here are productive.

The definitive meta-analysis compared distributed against massed practice at equal total study time, across hundreds of direct comparisons. Commit to a guess: how lopsided was the tally, and roughly how big was the recall difference?

You need to still know something ten weeks from now. Commit to a guess at how long the gap between your first and second study session should be, and say what you based the number on.

Why this lesson

Lesson 3 said what to do with your study time. This lesson is about when, and the when turns out not to be arbitrary. It also settles a question the first three lessons keep raising without answering: given that a delayed test is the only honest measure, how long is the delay supposed to be?

The gap scales with your retention horizon

Distributed practice beats massed practice for equal total study time in 259 of 271 direct comparisons, across 839 assessments and 317 experiments, with overall recall of 47.3% spaced against 36.7% massed (Cepeda et al. 2006). Very little in this field is that uniform.

The more useful result is about the size of the gap. In a 1,354-participant experiment, optimal gaps were 1, 11, 21 and 21 days for retention intervals of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days, and the gain of the optimal gap over massed study was +64% recall, d = 1.1 (Cepeda et al. 2008). The planning ratio drawn from this is deliberately rough: about 10% to 30% of the retention interval at week-to-months horizons, falling to roughly 5% to 7% at a year. Treat it as a way of getting into the right order of magnitude, not as a formula.

Two asymmetries make the ratio usable in practice, and both are stated in that paper.

  • Spacing too little costs far more than spacing too much. Accuracy rises steeply as the gap grows toward the optimum, then declines only gradually past it. The curve is not symmetric, so when you are uncertain about your horizon, err longer.
  • Massed study produces high immediate mastery that will not survive substantial delay. This is Lesson 1's finding in its original setting. It means the condition that feels most efficient is the one whose feeling is least informative.

Expanding intervals are folklore, not evidence

The advice you will most often meet is a specific schedule shape: review after one day, then three, then nine, with each gap larger than the last. The shape is not what carries the effect.

Meta-analytically, expanding schedules perform no better than uniform spacing, g = 0.034, not statistically significant, while spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval strongly, g = 0.74 (Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021). In the classic direct comparison, expanding schedules won only at a ten-minute delay; equal spacing won at two days; and delaying the first test improved long-term retention regardless of what shape the subsequent schedule took (Karpicke & Roediger 2007).

So the wins come from three things, none of them geometric: spacing the retrievals at all, delaying the first one rather than reviewing immediately, and adapting to how the recalls actually go. If you have been trying and failing to hold a particular progression in your head, you have been guarding the one part of the schedule that does not matter.

One operational consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the most commonly violated. Missed sessions reschedule forward; they do not stack. Collapsing three skipped reviews into one sitting recreates massed practice, which is the condition all of this evidence is measured against.

Check your recall

Answers stay hidden until you commit an attempt. Three items from this lesson, one reaching back.

Give the headline numbers for the spacing effect from Cepeda and colleagues (2006), and state the feature of the experimental design that makes the result interesting rather than trivial.

You have a cumulative exam in twelve weeks and you are studying a body of material now. Work out roughly when to schedule your second and third passes, say which direction you should err if you are unsure, and give the reason that direction is the safe one.

What does the evidence say about expanding review schedules such as one day, three days, nine days, and what does it identify as the parts of a schedule that actually carry the benefit?

Write before we talk

Pick one thing you genuinely need to still know on a specific future date, and write the date down. Compute 10% and 30% of the interval between today and it, and write the two calendar dates that fall at those two points. Bring the three dates to the chat, along with what you would have scheduled by instinct instead.
